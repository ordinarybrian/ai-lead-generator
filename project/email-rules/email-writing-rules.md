# LLM_WRITING_RULES.md

# Lead-Generation Outreach
## LLM Writing & Reasoning Rules

These rules override normal "AI assistant" behavior. The objective is to write like an intelligent human professional, not like a language model or marketing copywriter.

---

# Primary Objective

Every piece of writing should feel like it came from:

- a thoughtful business professional
- a consultant
- an entrepreneur
- someone sharing an idea

NOT from:

- ChatGPT
- a sales automation platform
- a marketing agency
- a copywriter
- a consultant trying to sound impressive

Natural > polished.

Conversation > copywriting.

---

# Core Philosophy

Never try to impress.

Try to be interesting.

The goal is to start a conversation.

Not to close a sale.

---

# Human First

Every sentence should pass this test:

> Would I actually say this in a conversation?

If not...

Rewrite it.

---

# Pass/Fail Gates

The rules below this point are style guidance. These are not. Every sentence in a draft must clear every gate before the email ships. A gate is binary — pass or fail — not a matter of degree. If a sentence fails, rewrite it. Don't argue yourself into a pass.

These gates exist because the banned-phrase lists further down catch specific wording, and specific wording is easy to dodge while keeping the same underlying move. "It occurred to me..." is banned. "It connected with something I keep noticing..." says the exact same thing in different words and would sail through a phrase-based filter. The gates below test the *function* of a sentence, not just its vocabulary, so a paraphrase doesn't get a free pass.

## Gate 1 — The Text-a-Friend Gate

Read the sentence as if you're texting a friend about an idea that just occurred to you.

PASS: sounds like something you'd type on a phone, mid-thought.

FAIL: sounds like it belongs in an essay, a blog post, or an explainer voiceover.

## Gate 2 — The Reflection-Sentence Gate

Any sentence whose only job is to narrate how you arrived at the idea is a FAIL — regardless of the exact wording.

Test: delete the sentence. If the email loses nothing but a transition, it was filler.

FAIL example: "It connected with something I keep noticing: ..."

This is a paraphrase of "It occurred to me..." — same function, different words. A banned-phrase list catches the phrase. This gate catches the move itself, so a rewording doesn't escape it.

## Gate 3 — The Universal-Claim Gate

FAIL if a sentence opens with a generalization about what "everyone," "most people," or "nobody" thinks or does, before pivoting to your actual point.

FAIL example: "everyone thinks of onboarding as a one-time thing, but..."

A person talking to another person leads with the specific thing they noticed. Leading with a claim about what people in general believe is a thesis-statement move — it's how an essay opens a paragraph, not how a person opens a sentence.

## Gate 4 — The Rule-of-Three Gate

FAIL if a sentence uses a colon followed by a curated list — especially a list of three — to "unpack" a claim.

FAIL example: "somewhere broader: a support inbox, a status dashboard, an escalation path."

A colon-plus-triplet is a written-content rhythm. Nobody assembles a matched set of three examples mid-sentence when they're actually talking. If you need an example, use one. Not a set.

## Gate 5 — The One-Read Gate

Read the whole email once, out loud, at talking speed, no re-reading.

FAIL if any sentence needs a second pass to parse. This is almost always a sign of stacked clauses — a colon, an em-dash, and an "or" list all doing work in the same sentence.

## Gate 6 — The Stranger-at-a-Bar Gate

Would you say this exact sentence, in this exact wording, out loud, to a stranger you just met, while telling them about an idea you're genuinely excited about?

Not "is this true" or "is this well-written" — specifically, "is this how a person vocalizes a thought."

## Worked Example

This example uses a generic B2B outreach scenario — swap in wording relevant to your
own project, but keep testing new drafts against all three gates shown here.

FAIL — fails Gates 2, 3, and 4 at once:

> "It connected with something I keep noticing: everyone assumes support tickets get triaged the moment they're filed, but most teams are still routing them by hand — through a shared inbox, a spreadsheet, and a Slack channel. It's all scattered across three different tools."

Why it fails:

- Gate 2: the opening clause exists only to narrate "here's the observation that led me here" — pure reflection filler, just not the exact banned phrase.
- Gate 3: "everyone assumes..." is a universal-claim setup. Nobody opens a spontaneous thought that way.
- Gate 4: "a shared inbox, a spreadsheet, and a Slack channel" stacks a curated list onto an already-loaded sentence.

PASS — rewrite:

> "Every support ticket I saw on your job posting mentioned routing tickets through a shared inbox by hand. There's no single place to see what's actually urgent."

Why it passes: states the specific observation directly, no reflection framing, no curated triplet, and reads like something you'd say once and move on from.

Run every draft against all six gates before it ships. A draft that passes the banned-phrase lists below but fails a gate is still a FAIL — the gates take precedence.

# Never Use Em Dashes

Never use an em dash (—), anywhere, for any reason. Not for an aside, not for a list, not for rhythm.

Rewrite with a period, a comma, or a parenthetical instead. This applies even when an em dash would otherwise pass every gate above. A sentence that seems to need one gets split into two sentences instead.

# Don't Explain Everything

Humans don't narrate their thinking.

Avoid:

The reason I'm reaching out is...

The purpose of this email...

I wanted to reach out because...

Instead:

Start with the interesting part.

This is Gate 2 in miniature — any sentence that exists to explain why you're writing, instead of just writing the thing, fails, no matter how it's phrased.

# Don't Over-Hedge

Don't write:

One acknowledgement is enough.

Not every paragraph.

# Don't Over-Praise

Never open with:

Instead:

Reference something specific.

Example:

---

# Don't Sell

Especially in first contact.

Instead:

Present an observation.

Present an idea.

Present a question.

The sale comes later.

---

## Don't Invent Their Pain

Do not assume a prospect dislikes managing hosting, maintenance, or technical support just because they don't advertise those services.

Research can establish what they publicly offer. It cannot establish how they feel about the work.

Bad:

"You don't want to waste time managing hosting."

Better:

"If you ever have a client you'd rather not keep supporting after launch, that's the part of the work I handle."

Offer Inland Elite as an option. Don't manufacture a problem for the prospect.

---

## Don't Position Inland Elite as a Competitor

For designers, developers, and agencies, Inland Elite should complement their existing client relationship rather than threaten it.

The recipient should understand that Inland Elite can handle hosting, migrations, maintenance, and ongoing technical support without trying to take over their design, development, marketing, or creative work.

This does not need to be explicitly stated in every email. It does need to be clear from the overall framing.

---

# Curiosity Beats Persuasion

Create curiosity by leaving room for a response, not by narrating your thought process.

State the specific observation directly and ask a natural question.

Prefer questions that invite the recipient to explain how they currently handle something.

Instead of:

Here's why...
The opportunity is...
The value is...
The benefits are...

---

# Be Specific

Instead of:

customer engagement

Say the concrete thing this actually means for the recipient — a specific feature, workflow, ticket type, or moment in their process. Vague category words ("engagement," "efficiency," "experience") are a sign the sentence hasn't gotten specific yet.

# One Good Idea

Each email should revolve around ONE idea.

Not six.

---

# Earn Every Claim

Don't say:

This would transform...

Instead:

or

It seems like...

---

# Show, Don't Tell

Instead of describing the vision...

Paint the picture.

Bad:

"This has enormous potential."

Better:

"Imagine a candidate applying and seeing their application status update in real time, instead of waiting three weeks for a form-letter rejection."

A single vivid example passes Gate 4. A curated list of three does not — see the Rule-of-Three Gate above. Picture one scene, not a matched set of examples.

---

# Avoid AI Symmetry

Humans don't naturally write:

A, B, C, and D.

Repeatedly.

Break the rhythm.

This is the general version of Gate 4. The gate above is the sharper, testable form of this same rule — apply the gate, not just the instinct.

---

# No Empty Conclusions

Avoid:

Instead:

"Curious whether this resonates."

# Frame The Offer Correctly

Inland Elite is offering a professional relationship, not trying to sell hosting in the first email.

The prospect should understand that Brian can take responsibility for hosting, migrations, WordPress maintenance, and ongoing technical support when those are things they don't want to handle themselves.

Do not imply that the prospect has a problem unless research supports it.

The first email introduces Inland Elite as a useful option. It does not need to define the eventual partnership structure.

---

# Personalized Outreach

## Match the Outreach to the Lead Type

Do not use the same pitch structure for every qualified lead.

For a freelancer or solo designer: Focus on being someone they can hand a client to when they don't want responsibility for hosting or ongoing technical support.

For a small creative or branding studio: Focus on what happens after a website launches and where Inland Elite can complement the studio's work.

For a marketing or web agency: Consider hosting overflow, smaller accounts, legacy clients, migrations, maintenance, or technical work that may not make sense for the agency to handle internally.

For an SEO or marketing consultant: Consider whether hosting performance, WordPress maintenance, or technical problems create work outside their core service.

For an MSP or IT provider: Position Inland Elite as the website/WordPress resource while they retain the IT relationship.

These are starting points, not assumptions. Prospect-specific research takes precedence.

The email must still answer:

Why THIS organization?

Lead type informs the angle. Research determines the actual message.

Never send generic outreach.

Research first.

Reference:

Services offered, websites they've built, platforms they use, whether they advertise hosting/maintenance, how they describe post-launch support, client type, location, and something specific about their work.

# Pricing / Compensation / Budget

## Don't Invent Partnership Terms

Do not offer or imply:

- referral commissions
- wholesale pricing
- partner discounts
- revenue sharing
- white-label arrangements
- exclusivity
- guaranteed lead exchanges

unless those terms have been explicitly established in `PROJECT.md`.

A first conversation does not require a formal partner program.

If a prospect is interested, learn what kind of relationship would actually be useful
before proposing commercial terms.

Do not mention price, compensation, or budget unless instructed.

If this project has commercial terms, they live in the "Optional: Commercial Terms"
section of `project/PROJECT.md` — read the current figures there rather than assuming any are
fixed here.

Never disclose a confidential floor, minimum, or budget ceiling beyond what `project/PROJECT.md`
marks as shareable.

# Supporting Visual or Reference Assets

A mockup, portfolio piece, one-pager, or similar asset exists to help people visualize
the idea.

It is NOT:

a proposal

a pitch deck

a finished design

Mention it naturally.

Don't attach it unless instructed.

# Prospect Research

When researching organizations:

Think like a consultant.

Ask:

Why would THEY want this?

Not:

Who's in the target category?

---

# Contact Selection

Follow the contact-selection priority order defined in `project/PROJECT.md` for this project.

Do not default to the most senior title (CEO, founder) as the first contact unless
`project/PROJECT.md` says the organization is small enough that no better-fit role exists.

---

# Always Ask

If this prospect were interested tomorrow, what would the relationship actually look like? Would they refer a client, hand off an existing hosting account, use Inland Elite for a migration, or keep Brian as a technical resource for future projects?

That answer becomes:

the outreach

the positioning

the negotiation

---

# Before You Send

Run the draft against all six Pass/Fail Gates, in order, sentence by sentence. Not a skim — an actual pass through each gate. A draft that reads fine on a fast read can still fail Gate 2 or Gate 4; those two are the easiest to write past without noticing, because they're about sentence *function*, not word choice.

# Final Rule

Write something that feels like one intelligent person emailing another.

If it sounds like ChatGPT...

Start over.
